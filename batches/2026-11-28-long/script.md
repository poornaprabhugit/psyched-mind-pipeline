## 0 · Cold open
> **VO:** It's Saturday morning. Pip has fifty dollars in his wallet and one rule for the day: spend nothing he doesn't need.
> He even writes it down. Spend nothing. He sticks the note right on his wallet, where he can't miss it.
> By tonight, the fifty dollars will be gone. And here's the strange part. Pip will feel fine about every single choice he made.
> He won't feel tricked. He won't feel careless. He'll feel smart.
> Eight times today, his brain will play a trick on him. Each trick has been tested in real studies, with real people and real money.
> So here's the game. Watch Pip's day, and try to spot each trick before he does. Keep count. At the end, we'll find out why none of it felt like losing.

*Animation:* Pip wakes up, counts bills, sticks "SPEND NOTHING" on the wallet. Title card. A "TRICKS 0/8" counter appears top right and stays all video. Small chapter label top left.

## 1 · The free stamps (endowed progress)
> **VO:** First stop: coffee. Pip only wants one small coffee. The barista smiles and hands him a loyalty card. Collect ten stamps, get a free coffee. And look, two stamps are already filled in. A little gift.
> Pip's brain says: I've already started. I'm two stamps in.
> Researchers tested exactly this with loyalty cards. One group got a card that needed eight stamps. Another group got a card that needed ten stamps, but two were already filled in for free.
> Think about that. Both groups had to buy the same eight more to finish. The work was identical.
> But the card with the free head start was finished almost twice as often.
> The only thing that changed was how far along it felt. The brain doesn't just ask how much is left. It asks how much it has already done. And a head start you didn't earn still counts.
> You see this everywhere. Progress bars that start at twenty percent. Apps that tell you your profile is almost complete. Rewards cards that come pre-stamped.
> Pip smiles, looks at his card, and orders a second coffee. Just for progress. Trick number one.

*Gag:* sparkle on the two stamps; Pip with two coffees, one in each hand, looking proud. Counter 1/8.
*Source:* Nunes & Drèze (2006), JCR.

## 2 · The gym (paying for future you)
> **VO:** On his way, Pip walks past his gym. He pays for a monthly membership. This month, he's been twice.
> He doesn't feel bad about it. In his head, the membership isn't for this month's Pip. It's for next month's Pip. The fit one.
> A study looked at almost eight thousand gym members. People on monthly plans paid over seventy dollars a month, and went about four times.
> That's more than seventeen dollars for each visit. The same gym sold a pass for ten visits at ten dollars a visit.
> About eighty percent of the monthly members would have saved money just by paying per visit.
> They chose the monthly plan because they expected to go a lot more. They weren't paying for the gym they used. They were paying for the person they planned to become.
> And that's the trick. Paying for a plan feels like you've already done something good. The plan gives you the feeling, even when the visits never come.
> Pip waves at the gym. Next month, he tells himself. Trick number two.

*Gag:* thought bubble: buff "future Pip" lifting a dumbbell; real Pip trips over the doormat. On screen: "$70+/month ÷ 4 visits = $17+ per visit" vs "10-visit pass: $10". Counter 2/8.
*Source:* DellaVigna & Malmendier (2006), AER.

## 3 · The ski trip (sunk cost)
> **VO:** Pip checks his phone. Bad news. Two trips he booked for next weekend overlap. A big ski trip he paid one hundred dollars for. And a smaller one he paid fifty dollars for, that he knows he'd enjoy more.
> He can only go on one. The money is already spent either way. Which one should he choose?
> Researchers asked people this exact question. More than half chose the one-hundred-dollar trip, the one they expected to enjoy less.
> Why? Because skipping the expensive one feels like throwing away a hundred dollars. But that money is gone no matter what. The only real choice left is which weekend he'll enjoy more.
> This is the sunk cost trap. Finishing a movie you hate because you paid for the ticket. Eating everything on the plate because it was expensive. The money already spent keeps pulling you, even though it can't come back.
> Pip sighs and picks the expensive trip. Trick number three.

*Gag:* two tickets on the phone, the "$100" one sits on a scale like a heavy weight; Pip chooses it and gets a tiny snow cloud over his head. Counter 3/8.
*Source:* Arkes & Blumer (1985), OBHDP.

## 4 · The price that ends in 9 (9-endings)
> **VO:** At the store, Pip sees a jacket. Thirty-four dollars. He walks on. He doesn't need a jacket.
> A week later, the same jacket is thirty-nine dollars. And now, somehow, it looks like a deal.
> That sounds silly. But in real catalog experiments, the same dress sold more at thirty-nine dollars than at thirty-four.
> And raising it to forty-four didn't beat thirty-nine.
> A price ending in nine has become a signal. It says sale, it says good value, even when it's the higher price. The brain reads the signal before it does the math.
> That's why so many prices end in ninety-nine, in dollars, rupees or pounds.
> Pip buys the jacket. Trick number four.

*Gag:* tag flips $34 → $39, a tiny "SALE" halo pops over the 9, Pip's eyes turn into hearts. Counter 4/8.
*Source:* Anderson & Simester (2003), QME.

## 5 · FREE (zero price)
> **VO:** At the checkout, there's a small bowl of chocolates. A fancy truffle for fifteen cents. A cheap chocolate for one cent.
> In a real experiment, most people chose the truffle. Seventy-three percent. It's clearly the better deal for a little more.
> Then the researchers made both one cent cheaper. Fourteen cents for the truffle. And the cheap chocolate became free.
> The difference between them was exactly the same as before. But now sixty-nine percent took the free one.
> Free doesn't feel like a low price. It feels like a different kind of thing, a choice with no downside at all. So people stop comparing and just grab it.
> Free shipping if you spend a little more. Buy one, get one free. A free gift with your order. The word free quietly switches off the comparing.
> Pip grabs the free chocolate. And because shipping on his phone order was free above fifty dollars, he adds one more thing to the cart. Trick number five.

*Gag:* a crowd of beans walks to the truffle, then all U-turn at once when FREE appears; the truffle sighs. Counter 5/8.
*Source:* Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007), Marketing Science.

## 6 · The pocket change (denomination effect)
> **VO:** Pip gets his change. Not a dollar bill. Four quarters.
> In one study, people were given one dollar and a chance to spend it. When the dollar came as four quarters, sixty-three percent spent it. When it came as a single bill, only twenty-six percent did.
> Same dollar. Very different behavior.
> A whole bill feels like something to protect. Breaking it feels like a loss. But small change feels like it's already been spent, like it doesn't really count.
> It's the same with small payments on your phone. A little here, a little there. Each one feels too small to matter.
> Pip's quarters roll straight into a candy machine. Trick number six.

*Gag:* four quarters roll out of the pocket one by one into a candy machine; a folded bill stays in the wallet behind a tiny padlock. Counter 6/8.
*Source:* Raghubir & Srivastava (2009), JCR.

## 7 · The lost ticket (mental accounting)
> **VO:** Evening. Pip bought a ten-dollar ticket to a show. He gets to the door, reaches into his pocket, and the ticket is gone.
> Would you buy another one?
> In a classic study, people were asked to imagine two versions of this night. In one, they lost a ten-dollar bill on the way to the theater. Eighty-eight percent said they'd still buy a ticket.
> In the other, they lost the ten-dollar ticket itself. Only forty-six percent said they'd buy another.
> It's the same ten dollars either way. So why the difference?
> Because the brain doesn't keep one big account. It keeps little jars. A lost bill comes out of the general jar. But a lost ticket comes out of the show jar. Buy another, and in your head, the show now costs twenty dollars. Suddenly it feels too expensive.
> Pip doesn't buy another ticket. Then he spends twelve dollars on snacks instead, from a different jar. Trick number seven.

*Gag:* pocket check, both pockets pop out inside-out, a puff of dust; jars labelled SHOW and SNACKS; the SHOW jar's price tag reads $20. Counter 7/8.
*Source:* Tversky & Kahneman (1981), Science.

## 8 · The old mug (endowment effect)
> **VO:** Back home, Pip decides to make some money back. He'll sell his old mug online. He asks seven dollars for it.
> Nobody buys.
> In a well-known experiment, some students were given a mug. Others were not. Then they could trade. The students who owned the mug wanted about seven dollars to sell it. The students without one would only pay about three dollars for the very same mug.
> Same mug, same room, same day. The only difference was who owned it.
> The moment something becomes ours, it seems to be worth more. That's why selling your old things is so frustrating. Once it's yours, giving it up feels like losing something.
> Pip hugs his mug. Trick number eight.

*Gag:* the mug gets a halo and a tiny crown; buyer bubbles say "$3"; Pip hugs the mug. Counter 8/8.
*Source:* Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990), JPE.

## 9 · The answer (loss aversion + the jars)
> **VO:** So, back to our question. Pip lost money eight times today. Why didn't any of it feel like losing?
> First, the jars. Pip's brain doesn't watch one big account. It watches many small ones. Coffee. Gym. Trips. Clothes. Snacks. Each choice was judged inside its own little jar, so none of them ever looked big.
> Second, losses. Across more than a hundred studies, a loss weighs about twice as much as a gain of the same size. Not for everyone, and not always. People differ a lot. But on average, losing ten dollars hurts about as much as winning twenty feels good.
> And look at today's tricks again. The stamps felt like a gain. The free chocolate felt like a gain. The thirty-nine-dollar jacket felt like a deal. Skipping the hundred-dollar ski trip felt like a loss, so he avoided it. Selling his mug for less felt like a loss, so he refused.
> Every single trick felt either like a small win, or like dodging a loss. Eight small wins. One empty wallet.

*Animation:* the day's jars line up in a row, each with a small amount; then they pour into one big jar labelled "TODAY" that ends up empty. A see-saw: "LOSS" weighs about twice "GAIN". Pip opens his wallet: a moth flies out.
*Source:* Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2024), JEL (meta-analysis, ~1.96×).

## 10 · The twist (buying time) + outro
> **VO:** There's one more study worth knowing.
> In one study, working adults spent forty dollars on two different days. On one day, they spent it on something that saved them time, like paying someone to do a chore. On the other day, they spent it on a thing for themselves.
> They felt happier on the day they bought time.
> So maybe the question isn't only how much did I spend. It's also what did I get back. Pip got a second coffee, a jacket and some snacks. He didn't get a single minute back.
> Next Saturday, Pip will start with fifty dollars again. And his brain will try the same eight tricks.
> Now you know them. Which one gets you the most? Tell me in the comments. And if this changed how you see your own wallet, send it to the friend who always says, but it was on sale.

*Gag:* Pip relaxes on the couch while a tiny robot vacuum does the chore, a clock icon with "+1 hour" floats up, Pip smiles. Final shot: the "SPEND NOTHING" note, the moth lands on it; Pip stares at the camera, deadpan. End screen.
*Source:* Whillans et al. (2017), PNAS.
