## 0 · Cold open (≈ 0:00–0:35)
> **VO:** It's Saturday morning. Pip has fifty dollars and one rule: today, he spends nothing he doesn't need.
> By tonight, the fifty dollars will be gone. And here's the strange part: Pip will feel fine about every single choice.
> Seven times today, his brain will trick him. Seven real studies show how. Try to spot each trick before Pip does.

*Animation:* Pip wakes up, counts a fat stack of bills, puts a sticky note on the wallet: "SPEND NOTHING." Title card. A "TRICKS: 0/7" counter appears in the corner and stays all video.

## 1 · The free stamps (≈ 0:35–1:25) — endowed progress
> **VO:** First stop: coffee. The barista hands Pip a loyalty card. Ten stamps for a free coffee — and two are already stamped, as a gift.
> Pip's brain says: I'm already on my way.
> Researchers tested exactly this with loyalty cards. One group got a card with eight stamps to fill. Another got a card with ten stamps, but two were already filled in. Same eight washes to go.
> The card with the free head start was finished almost twice as often.
> Nothing changed, except how far along it felt. Trick number one: a head start you didn't earn still pulls you forward.

*Gag:* Pip looks at the card, dramatic sparkle, he orders a second coffee "for progress". Counter → 1/7.
*Source:* Nunes & Drèze (2006), JCR.

## 2 · The gym (≈ 1:25–2:20) — paying for future you
> **VO:** Next, Pip walks past his gym. He pays for a monthly membership. He's been twice this month.
> A study of almost eight thousand gym members found people on monthly plans paid over seventy dollars a month — and went about four times.
> That's more than seventeen dollars a visit. A ten-visit pass cost ten dollars a visit.
> About eighty percent of monthly members would have saved money paying per visit.
> They weren't paying for the gym they used. They were paying for the person they planned to become. Trick number two.

*Gag:* Pip waves at the gym, a thought bubble shows a buff "future Pip" lifting weights; real Pip trips over the doormat. Counter → 2/7.
*Source:* DellaVigna & Malmendier (2006), AER.

## 3 · The price that ends in 9 (≈ 2:20–3:10) — 9-endings
> **VO:** At the store, Pip sees a jacket for thirty-four dollars. Next week, the same jacket is thirty-nine.
> You'd think the cheaper price wins. In real catalog tests, the same dress sold better at thirty-nine dollars than at thirty-four.
> And forty-four didn't beat thirty-nine.
> A price ending in nine whispers "deal", even when it's higher. Trick number three.

*Gag:* Price tag flips from $34 to $39; a tiny "SALE" halo appears over the 9; Pip's eyes turn into hearts. Counter → 3/7.
*Source:* Anderson & Simester (2003), QME.

## 4 · FREE (≈ 3:10–4:05) — zero price
> **VO:** At the checkout, two chocolates. A fancy truffle for fifteen cents, a cheap chocolate for one cent. Most people chose the truffle — seventy-three percent.
> Then both got one cent cheaper. Fourteen cents, or free.
> Now sixty-nine percent grabbed the free one.
> The difference between the prices never changed. But "free" doesn't feel like a lower price. It feels like no risk at all. Trick number four.

*Gag:* A crowd of beans walks to the truffle, then all U-turn to the FREE sign at once; the truffle sighs. Counter → 4/7.
*Source:* Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007), Marketing Science.

## 5 · The pocket change (≈ 4:05–4:50) — denomination effect
> **VO:** Pip gets his change in coins. Four quarters.
> In one study, people got one dollar. Given as four quarters, sixty-three percent spent it. Given as a single dollar bill, only twenty-six percent did.
> Same dollar. A bill feels like money to protect. Coins feel like money that's already half gone. Trick number five.

*Gag:* The four quarters roll out of Pip's pocket one by one into a candy machine; the bill stays tucked away, guarded by a tiny padlock. Counter → 5/7.
*Source:* Raghubir & Srivastava (2009), JCR.

## 6 · The lost ticket (≈ 4:50–5:45) — mental accounting
> **VO:** Evening. Pip bought a ten-dollar ticket to a show. At the door — it's gone.
> Would you buy another one?
> In a classic study, people who lost a ten-dollar bill on the way still bought a ticket — eighty-eight percent. People who lost the ticket itself? Only forty-six percent bought another.
> Same ten dollars, either way. But the brain keeps money in separate jars. Lose the ticket, and the "show" jar now says twenty dollars. Trick number six.

*Gag:* Pocket check — both pockets pop out inside-out, a moth flies out. Two jars labelled "SHOW" and "FOOD"; a coin moves from one to the other and the SHOW jar shows $20. Counter → 6/7.
*Source:* Tversky & Kahneman (1981), Science.

## 7 · The old mug (≈ 5:45–6:30) — endowment effect
> **VO:** Back home, Pip tries to sell an old mug online. He asks seven dollars.
> In a famous experiment, students who were given a mug wanted about seven dollars to sell it. Students without one would only pay about three dollars for the very same mug.
> The moment something is ours, it feels worth more. Trick number seven.

*Gag:* Pip hugs the mug; the mug gets a halo and a tiny crown; buyers' offer bubbles say "$3". Counter → 7/7.
*Source:* Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990), JPE.

## 8 · The answer (≈ 6:30–7:10) — loss aversion ties it together
> **VO:** So why didn't Pip feel a single loss today?
> Because his brain doesn't keep one big account. It keeps little jars — coffee, gym, jacket, show, mug — and judges each one alone.
> And it fears losses more than it enjoys gains. Across more than a hundred studies, a loss weighs about twice as much as a gain of the same size — though people differ a lot.
> Each trick felt like a small win, or like avoiding a loss. Seven small wins. One empty wallet.
> Which trick got you? Tell me in the comments.

*Gag:* Pip opens the wallet: one moth flies out, does a little loop, and lands on the "SPEND NOTHING" note. Pip stares at the camera, deadpan. End card.
*Source:* Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2024), JEL (meta-analysis, ~1.96×).
