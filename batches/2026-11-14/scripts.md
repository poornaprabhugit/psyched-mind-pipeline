## s01 · wealth · Mon 16 Nov 12:00 · predicted medium
**Title:** Why winning millions wouldn't make you happier 💰  
**Hook card:** Won millions. Not happier.  
**Ending:** use-it  
**Source:** Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology – https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.36.8.917

Lottery winners weren't happier than people who won nothing. Scientists interviewed them. Worse: everyday pleasures, a chat with a friend, a good breakfast, felt smaller to them. Your brain gets used to anything, even millions. So the small joys you already have? They're worth more than you think.

## s02 · wealth · Tue 17 Nov 16:00 · predicted high
**Title:** Why you'd choose the worse holiday 🎿  
**Hook card:** $100 trip vs $50 trip?  
**Ending:** use-it question  
**Source:** Arkes & Blumer (1985), Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes – https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4

More than half of people picked the worse holiday, because it cost more. Two ski trips, same weekend: a hundred dollars, or fifty. The fifty-dollar one is more fun. They still chose the expensive one. The money's gone either way, but your brain hates wasting it. So ask: if both were free, which would I pick?

## s03 · everyday · Wed 18 Nov 12:00 · predicted medium
**Title:** You'd defend a choice you never made 🤯  
**Hook card:** You picked A. They gave you B.  
**Ending:** question  
**Source:** Johansson, Hall, Sikström & Olsson (2005), Science – https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1111709

You'd defend a choice you never made. Scientists secretly swapped the face people had picked as more attractive. Most didn't notice. They even explained why they chose it. One man praised her earrings. The face he'd actually chosen had none. Your brain invents reasons after you decide. So how sure are you about your last choice?

## s04 · wealth · Thu 19 Nov 16:00 · predicted medium
**Title:** Why your coffee card comes with free stamps ☕  
**Hook card:** 8 stamps vs 10 with 2 free  
**Ending:** use-it  
**Source:** Nunes & Drèze (2006), Journal of Consumer Research – https://doi.org/10.1086/500480

Two free stamps almost doubled how many people finished their loyalty card. Researchers tested it at a car wash. One card: eight stamps needed. The other: ten, with two already stamped. Same eight washes. Your brain hates leaving things half-done. Businesses use it. You can too: give your next goal a head start.

## s05 · health · Fri 20 Nov 12:00 · predicted medium
**Title:** Why you snap after a bad night's sleep 😴  
**Hook card:** No sleep = 60% more reactive  
**Ending:** use-it  
**Source:** Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz & Walker (2007), Current Biology – https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007

One sleepless night, and your brain's alarm centre reacts sixty percent more. Scientists kept people awake a night and a day, then showed them upsetting pictures in a brain scanner. Compared with people who'd slept, the part that calms it down was less connected. So that argument after a bad night? Sleep on it first.

## s06 · everyday · Sat 21 Nov 16:00 · predicted medium
**Title:** Why you hear your name in a noisy room 👂  
**Hook card:** You're listening without knowing it  
**Ending:** loop  
**Source:** Wood & Cowan (1995), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition – https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.21.1.255 (replicating Moray, 1959)

You hear your name even in conversations you're ignoring. Scientists played a different voice into each ear and asked people to repeat only one. A third still caught their own name in the other ear. Part of your brain keeps scanning the noise for what matters to you. That's why, in a loud room, you hear your name.

## s07 · wealth · Sun 22 Nov 12:00 · predicted high
**Title:** The happiest way to spend $40 isn't on stuff 💸  
**Hook card:** $40 on stuff vs $40 on time  
**Ending:** high-point line  
**Source:** Whillans, Dunn, Smeets, Bekkers & Norton (2017), PNAS – https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114

Spending forty dollars on time made people happier than spending it on stuff. Researchers gave working adults forty dollars, twice. Once for something material. Once to buy time: a cleaner, a delivery, a ride. Yet hardly anyone spends extra money that way. Rich isn't more stuff. It's more time.

# Long video 02 – The Psychology of Looking Rich (7 Real Studies)

## L0 · Opening (0:00–0:45)

The most expensive handbag in the shop is often the one with the smallest logo. And the people most eager to look rich tend to buy the loudest one. That's not a fashion rule. It's psychology, and researchers have measured it. In this video you'll see seven real studies about looking rich. How a price tag changes what you taste, why people treat you differently in a logo shirt, why a fake designer item can change how honest you are, and the one question that shows most of us would accept less money, just to feel richer. Stick around for number six. It's the one people argue about most. First, an experiment that put wine drinkers inside a brain scanner.

## L1 · 1 · The price tag you can taste

Number one: the price tag you can taste. Researchers at Caltech gave volunteers sips of wine while they lay inside a brain scanner. They were told they would taste five different Cabernet Sauvignons, and they saw the price of each one. But there were really only three wines. Two of them came back twice: once with a cheap price, once with an expensive one. A wine labelled forty-five dollars was the same wine they had also tasted labelled five dollars. You can guess the ratings. People said the expensive version tasted better. But here's what surprised the researchers: it wasn't just talk. When people believed the wine was expensive, a brain area linked to pleasure became more active. Same liquid, same tongue, a different experience in the brain. And the basic taste areas of the brain didn't change. The difference was in how much they enjoyed it. Your brain doesn't just measure what's in the glass. It predicts how good something will be, and price is one of its strongest clues. Then it partly delivers the experience it predicted. That's why the expensive version of almost anything can feel better, even when it isn't. Try this today: next time you compare two versions of something, cover the price first. Decide which you prefer, then look. But price isn't the only thing that makes something feel valuable. Sometimes all it takes is having less of it.

## L2 · 2 · Why rare feels rich

Number two: why rare feels rich. In a classic experiment, people were asked to taste a cookie and rate it. Some took their cookie from a jar holding ten cookies. Others from a jar holding just two. Same cookies, same recipe. The cookies from the nearly empty jar were rated as more desirable and more valuable. And it got stronger when the jar went from full to almost empty in front of people, as if others had grabbed them first. Those cookies were rated highest of all. Your brain treats scarcity as information. If only a few are left, something must be good about them, and someone else wants them too. For most of human history, that was a smart shortcut. Luxury brands understand this better than anyone: waiting lists, limited editions, only three left in stock. Think about the last time a booking site told you only two rooms were left. Did the room suddenly look nicer? The thing itself hasn't changed. The jar just looks emptier. Try this today: when you feel the pull of 'only a few left', ask yourself whether you'd still want it if the shelf were full. Scarcity changes how you see things. The next study shows how other people see you, depending on what you wear.

## L3 · 3 · The logo effect

Number three: the logo effect. And keep watching, because number six turns this one upside down. Researchers in the Netherlands ran a set of experiments with one simple change: the same shirt, with or without a luxury brand logo. In one, people watched a video of a man in a job interview. Same man, same answers, same voice. The only difference was the small logo on his shirt. Not his skills, not his experience. And the viewers would probably have said the logo meant nothing to them. With the luxury logo, viewers rated him as more suitable for the job, and suggested a salary about nine percent higher. In another, a person wearing a logo sweater collected money for charity. With the logo, more people gave. Why? A luxury label is an expensive signal. It's costly to fake, so the brain reads it as a hint of wealth and status. And people with status are people we want on our side. Try this today: notice the next time you judge someone by a brand. You're reacting to a signal, not the person. And that brings us to the strange part: the quietest signals are often the richest.

## L4 · 4 · Why old money whispers

Number four: why old money whispers. Marketing researchers looked at luxury handbags from brands like Louis Vuitton and Gucci, and sorted them by how loudly they show the logo. Some have the brand printed all over. Others have a tiny logo, or none you'd spot. The quiet ones were, on average, the more expensive bags. Then they looked at who buys what. People with lots of money and no need to prove it preferred the quiet versions. They signal to a small group of insiders who recognise the design without a logo. People with money who want to be seen as rich preferred the loud versions. And people who want status but can't afford it were the most likely to buy counterfeits, almost always of the loud kind. The researchers called the quiet group patricians. Old money. Your brain reads a giant logo as 'look at me', and the people at the very top know it. So looking rich has a strange rule: the more you have, the less you need to show. That's why a watch with no visible logo can say more, to the right people, than one covered in diamonds. Try this today: think of the last thing you bought partly so others would notice. Would a quieter version have done the job? But if loud signals aren't the top of the ladder, what is? The next study found something stranger: breaking the rules.

## L5 · 5 · The red sneakers effect

Number five: the red sneakers effect. And stay for number seven: it's a single question, and most people's answer surprises them. Researchers went to luxury boutiques in Milan, the kind of shops with a doorman. They described two shoppers to the shop assistants. One walked in wearing a dress and a fur coat. The other walked in wearing gym clothes. Which one did the assistants think was the more important customer, more likely to buy? The one in gym clothes. In another study, students rated a professor at a top university. Described wearing red sneakers instead of formal shoes, he was judged as higher status and more competent. When someone breaks a dress code on purpose, your brain makes a quick calculation: they must have enough status to not care. Think of the founder in a hoodie at a board meeting. Your brain doesn't read sloppy. It reads: this person doesn't need to impress anyone. But there's a limit. It only worked when the rule-breaking looked deliberate, in a setting where people expected the rules. A professor in red sneakers at a top university: status. A random mismatched outfit: just a mistake. Try this today: you don't have to dress like money to be taken seriously. A confident, deliberate choice sends a stronger signal than a label. Which leads to the study that changed how researchers think about fake luxury.

## L6 · 6 · The counterfeit self

Number six: the counterfeit self. Researchers gave women real designer sunglasses, a brand that sells for hundreds of dollars. But some were told their pair came from a box labelled counterfeit. Others, from a box labelled authentic. In reality, every pair was genuine. Then the women wore the sunglasses while they solved maths puzzles, and graded their own work, with a chance to cheat for more money. Among women who thought their sunglasses were authentic, about thirty percent cheated. Among those who thought they were wearing fakes: seventy-one percent. The same glasses. Only the label in their head had changed. And it wasn't a fluke: with a new group, randomly assigned, the gap was almost the same. Sixty-nine percent against twenty-six. In follow-up studies, wearing the 'fakes' also made people more suspicious of others. The researchers' explanation: when you wear something that sends a false signal, part of you feels like a fake. And once you feel a bit fake, the next dishonest step feels smaller. Pretending to be richer than you are doesn't just risk your wallet. It can quietly change how you see yourself. Try this today: choose things that are honestly yours, even if they're simpler. Feeling genuine is worth more than looking expensive. And now, the question that shows how strange our relationship with money really is.

## L7 · 7 · Would you take less money to be richer?

Number seven: would you take less money to be richer? Researchers at Harvard asked students and staff to imagine two worlds, where prices are the same. In world A, you earn fifty thousand dollars a year, and everyone else earns twenty-five thousand. In world B, you earn one hundred thousand a year, and everyone else earns two hundred thousand. Which world do you choose? Take a second. In world B you'd have twice the money, and it buys exactly the same things. And yet about half the people chose world A. Half the money, as long as they were richer than everyone around them. And these were people at a school of public health: educated, thoughtful, answering a calm survey with no one watching. That's the engine behind everything in this video. Your brain doesn't measure wealth in dollars. It measures it in comparison. The wine, the cookies, the logo, the quiet bag, the red sneakers, even the fake sunglasses: they're all about where you stand next to other people. And the same survey found something telling: for holidays, most people chose more time off for themselves, even if others got more. Comparison hits hardest on the things other people can see. Try this today: the next time you feel poor, check who you're comparing yourself to. Compare with your own life a year ago instead. That's the one comparison that tells you if you're actually getting richer. If you want to see how the same brain loses money in everyday spending, watch this next: why your brain is bad with money.
